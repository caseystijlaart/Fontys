= Candidate Sensor Technologies

This chapter surveys non-camera sensing technologies capable of device-free person detection, localisation, and (where the literature supports it) height estimation, evaluated against the technical sub-questions in
xref:../../project-plan/project-assignment/research-questions.adoc[Project Plan - Research Questions], primarily the requirement for sufficient 3D spatial resolution to estimate a person's height, embedded-platform feasibility, and the data-minimisation/privacy constraints that follow from the primary visitors being children aged 8-12.
Distinguishing adult and child height is not a hard requirement at this stage.
A general height estimate is sufficient for now, with category classification treated as a possible refinement once a candidate technology is selected.

Each technology below is covered in two parts: what the literature has actually shown it can do, and, for the technically viable candidates, how that holds up once weighed against the criteria set out earlier, specific hardware modules, current cost, and pros and cons.
The chapter closes with a comparison summary, a criteria matrix across every approach discussed, and the resulting recommendation.

Ngamakeur et al. (<<ngamakeur2020survey,2020>>) survey device-free indoor localisation and tracking, framing this same comparison at a broader scale.
It structures device-free sensing into a pipeline (<<pipeline>>) that compares Wi-Fi, radar, infrared, and thermal approaches against each stage.
That pipeline view is used below to organise what each candidate technology has actually been shown to do in the literature, rather than what it could in principle do.

== Individual Position & Height Localisation

This section covers single-person, device-free position and height sensing, the core of RQ1.

[[time-of-flight-lidar]]
=== Time-of-Flight & LiDAR
2D LiDAR measures range by timing a laser pulse's reflection while scanning across a single horizontal plane, producing an (x, y) point cloud at the height the unit is mounted.
This makes it well suited to in-plane position and leg/torso-level tracking, but a single fixed-height scan plane does not by itself yield a height measurement.
Retrieving the height of an individual would require either multiple units mounted at different heights, an angled/tilted scan, or a full 3D ToF/LiDAR unit instead.

Within its native (x, y) tracking role, 2D LiDAR performs well: Bouazizi et al. (<<bouazizi2024multilidar,2024>>) fuse multiple 2D LiDAR units with a ConvLSTM model for indoor activity detection and show the approach tolerates furniture occlusion, a realistic condition for a room-scale interactive space.
At the single-scan-plane level, Enoki et al. (<<enoki2024waistlevel,2024>>) show waist-height contour data is sufficient for person identification and activity estimation using DBSCAN clustering with a deep learning classifier, which indicates the sensing modality carries enough per-person shape information for classification tasks even from one plane.
For the extensibility sub-question (RQ4), Alam et al. (<<palmar2021,2021>>) document, in PALMAR's related-work analysis, "crossover ambiguity" as the recurring failure mode of 2D LiDAR when multiple people's tracks pass close together, which is the concrete risk to plan around if this technology is carried forward into a multi-person scenario later.

On latency, the representative module identified below, the RPLIDAR A2M8, has a manufacturer-documented scan rate of 5-15 Hz (typically 10 Hz, i.e. 600 rpm), independently adjustable from its sample rate of up to roughly 4000 samples per second per Slamtec's datasheet.
This scan rate, not the faster per-sample rate, is what limits how often a full (x, y) frame around the sensor is available.

*Implication for RQ1:* 2D LiDAR alone does not produce a height estimate.
It would need to be paired with a second height-sensing modality or reconfigured (multi-height mounting, tilt-scanning, or a 3D ToF unit) to contribute a height signal, which should be weighed against its strong in-plane positional accuracy.
This chapter carries that pairing forward as part of <<combo-lidar-thermal,2D LiDAR + Thermal Fusion>>, below.

*Pros:*

* Strong in-plane position accuracy and furniture-occlusion tolerance when multiple units are fused (Bouazizi et al.).
* A single scan plane already carries enough per-person shape information for identification and activity classification (Enoki et al.), not just raw (x, y) position.
* High angular sampling rate (up to ~4000 samples/sec per Slamtec's datasheet), independent of the slower scan rate that paces full-frame updates.
* Mature, widely available consumer/robotics hardware (RPLIDAR family) with broad community and driver support.

*Cons:*

* Produces no height reading from a single fixed-height scan plane; needs multi-height mounting, a tilted scan, or a full 3D ToF/LiDAR unit to contribute a height signal at all.
* "Crossover ambiguity" is a documented failure mode when multiple people's tracks pass close together (Alam et al., PALMAR related work), a concrete risk for RQ4 multi-person extensibility.
* Scan rate of 5-15 Hz (typically 10 Hz) is on the slower end of the candidates reviewed, and sets the update-frequency ceiling regardless of the faster per-sample rate.
* Carrying height forward at all means accepting a second sensor (thermal, see <<combo-lidar-thermal,2D LiDAR + Thermal Fusion>>) or added mechanical complexity (multi-height mounting/tilt-scanning), not a same-sensor fix.

[[mmwave-radar]]
=== mmWave Radar

mmWave radar has an extensive body of research literature covering both position tracking and skeletal/joint-level pose estimation.
Point-cloud outputs from consumer mmWave radar are also inherently non-identifying (no recognisable imagery), which aligns with the ethical/legal sub-questions on minimising personally identifiable data collected from child visitors.

For position tracking, Huang et al. (<<huang2021mmwave,2021>>) combine clutter removal, clustering, and Kalman filtering for multi-person tracking running in real time on a Raspberry Pi 4.
Pegoraro and Rossi (<<pegoraro2022mmwave,2022>>) replace the Kalman filter with a CNN-RNN tracker with uncertainty estimation, reporting position error down to roughly 7.6 cm.

More directly relevant to the height-estimation requirement is the body of work on mmWave-based pose estimation: Zhu et al.'s ProbRadarM3F (<<probradarm3f2024,2024>>) recovers 14 body keypoints from radar point clouds via probability-map-guided multi-format feature fusion, and Zheng et al. (<<zheng2025mmpose,2025>>) survey related keypoint-estimation work (mmPose, mm-Pose, RadHAR) in their related-work section, giving a further citation trail if deeper comparison is needed.
Supporting datasets exist for this line of work: Su et al.'s mmFree-Pose dataset (<<su2024mmfreepose,2024>>) provides a camera-free radar dataset with 23-joint ground truth captured via motion capture, and Rahman et al.'s MMVR (<<rahman2024mmvr,2024>>) is a multi-view radar benchmark for indoor perception that references HuPR and other radar pose-estimation benchmarks.
As a keypoint output includes head/shoulder joints, this is the most direct existing path from raw sensor data to an actual height estimate, rather than a proxy such as blob size.

On the positioning-fundamentals side, Almalioglu et al.'s Milli-RIO (<<milliRio2019,2019>>) demonstrates centimetre-level ego-motion/indoor localisation using a single low-cost mmWave chip plus an IMU, which is a useful data point for embedded cost and compute feasibility (RQ2) independent of the pose-estimation question.

On latency specifically, the two position-tracking papers above report frame rates on the radar hardware they each actually used, rather than the TI IWR6843 recommended below: Pegoraro and Rossi (<<pegoraro2022mmwave,2022>>) report their INRAS RadarLog-based tracker outputting radar-Doppler maps at 15 fps (roughly 67 ms per frame), and Huang et al. (<<huang2021mmwave,2021>>) target a 50 ms (20 fps) frame budget on their IWR1642BOOST-based pipeline specifically so the Raspberry Pi 4 does not fall behind and drop frames.
For the IWR6843 chip itself, TI's datasheet supports frame rates up to roughly 76 Hz, but TI's own demo/application configurations typically run at 10-20 Hz (50-100 ms per frame); see the latency figures already established in Evaluation Criteria.
These are three different radar chips reporting broadly similar tens-of-milliseconds frame periods, which is a reasonable indication of what mmWave point-cloud tracking can achieve in general, but not a benchmark of the IWR6843 specifically.

*Implication for RQ1:* mmWave radar's literature includes published keypoint/pose-level output alongside demonstrated real-time embedded operation, and its device-free, non-identifying output profile fits the privacy sub-questions well.

Available product analysis narrows this to a 60 GHz point-cloud radar module built around the TI IWR6843 chip, from the same TI mmWave SDK/point-cloud family as the position-tracking literature above even though the exact chip cited differs paper to paper, over two alternatives considered and set aside: low-cost 24 GHz presence sensors (e.g. the HLK-LD2410 class of module), which output a binary presence flag rather than a point cloud and cannot support position or height estimation at all; and undocumented generic radar modules, which lack the reference software and community support needed to reach a working pipeline within the project timeline.
Several evaluation-board variants exist within the IWR6843 family itself, at different price points and field-of-view characteristics; <<mmwave-module-selection,mmWave Module Selection>> compares them directly and picks one to carry forward, so this chapter treats "TI IWR6843" as the candidate and defers the specific-variant decision to that chapter.

*Pros:*

* Strongest literature base of any candidate: real-time multi-person tracking (Huang et al.), keypoint-level pose/height estimation (Zhu et al., Zheng et al.).
* Demonstrated real-time operation on a Raspberry Pi 4.
* Point-cloud output is inherently non-identifying, fitting the project's data-minimisation requirements.
* Extends to the RQ4 active-speaker problem via the same sensor family (mmWave vibrometry), without a second dedicated device.
* Point clouds naturally separate multiple people, supporting future multi-person extensibility.

*Cons:*

* By far the most expensive candidate (€160-185 for the IWR6843AOPEVM variant compared in <<mmwave-module-selection,mmWave Module Selection>>, roughly three to four times the thermal array's sensor cost).
* Height estimation still requires extra work: a choice between a blob-size heuristic or a keypoint-extraction model, rather than a single established method.
* No project budget has yet been agreed with MindLabs, so this cost is a real, unresolved risk rather than a formality.

[[wifi-csi-individual]]
=== Wi-Fi CSI Sensing

Wi-Fi Channel State Information (CSI) sensing infers presence and movement from fluctuations in the wireless channel caused by a body moving through it.
It requires no dedicated sensor beyond commodity Wi-Fi hardware, which is attractive for cost, but the gathered literature is consistent that it trades away spatial resolution and stability.
CSI is sensitive to environmental changes (furniture, multipath, other RF sources), and reported positioning accuracy varies accordingly.

IndoTrack, by Li et al. (<<indotrack2017,2017>>), is the foundational device-free CSI tracking paper commonly cited in this space.
More recent work narrows toward this project's use case: a fingerprinting approach by Jiang and Yuan (<<jiang2025gmmwknn,2025>>) combines a Gaussian Mixture Model with weighted k-NN and Kalman filter tracking for device-free positioning.
Shen et al.'s time-selective RNN approach (<<shen2023timeselective,2023>>) directly addresses the busy-room, multi-person setting, and Chung et al.'s WiRiS (<<chung2023wiris,2023>>) uses a transformer architecture over RIS-assisted CSI to jointly solve people counting and localisation, which is the closest single-paper match to "how many people, where" as a joint problem.

Latency here depends heavily on the specific access point, not just the sensing algorithm: Shen et al. (<<shen2023timeselective,2023>>) report a CSI sampling rate of 10 Hz, explicitly capped by their TP-Link WDR4300 802.11n router rather than by their model, whereas IndoTrack is reported in secondary sources to have used a considerably faster 100 Hz commodity CSI stream, a figure this chapter could not independently confirm against the primary paper.
Chung et al. (<<chung2023wiris,2023>>) do not report any update rate or latency figure at all, evaluating WiRiS purely on counting/localisation accuracy.
This order-of-magnitude spread (10 Hz to 100 Hz) across otherwise comparable commodity Wi-Fi setups is itself a latency risk to flag for this criterion, separate from the modality's already-noted lack of height output.

*Implication for RQ1:* none of the gathered Wi-Fi CSI sources report height or pose-level output, and the modality's environment-sensitive spatial resolution limits its usefulness for producing a height estimate on its own.
It remains worth carrying forward as a low-cost option for presence/room-occupancy sensing.

*Pros:*

* Effectively free: reuses existing Wi-Fi infrastructure rather than needing dedicated hardware.
* Multi-person and multi-room literature exists (Time-Selective RNN, WiRiS).
* Device-free output with no recognisable imagery.

*Cons:*

* No published height-estimation precedent at all in the literature gathered for this chapter, the single biggest gap against RQ1.
* More sensitive to environmental changes (furniture, multipath) than radar or thermal.
* No demonstrated real-time embedded deployment in the sources reviewed.

[[ultrasonic-ranging]]
=== Ultrasonic Ranging

Active ultrasonic ranging measures distance via time-difference-of-arrival (TDOA) or time-of-flight on emitted acoustic pulses, rather than passively listening for ambient sound.
Because the system controls its own signal, this sidesteps the "room full of talking people" interference problem that a passive-listening approach would face.

Caicedo and Pandharipande (<<caicedoUltrasonic,2012>>) use a beamforming ultrasonic array with moving-target-indicator processing for indoor presence detection, and report it outperforming PIR sensors for occupancy detection.
Ranging resolution can be tight: Segers et al. (<<segers2015fskranging,2015>>) use frequency-shift-keyed ultrasound to track up to four simultaneous transmitters at 1-2cm accuracy on an FPGA, and Holm and Nilsen (<<holm2010ultrasonic,2010>>) demonstrate TDOA-based positioning from a transmitter array, a useful reference for multi-receiver node geometry if this technology is pursued further.
Wang et al. (<<ultrasonicVisionFusion2017,2017>>) fuse an ultrasonic array with vision for 3D human tracking, which is relevant mainly as a sensor-fusion architecture reference given this project's non-camera constraint.

Neither Segers et al. (<<segers2015fskranging,2015>>) nor Caicedo and Pandharipande (<<caicedoUltrasonic,2012>>) report an update rate, refresh rate, or end-to-end latency figure for their systems, only accuracy and, for Segers et al., the underlying ADC sampling rate used internally for correlation.
Latency for this modality is therefore an open question rather than a documented figure, an additional gap to close in any dedicated bench-testing pass.

*Implication for RQ1:* ultrasonic ranging gives decent range accuracy via TDOA/ToF, but azimuth resolution is limited, and full 2D/3D coverage needs multiple synchronised nodes, introducing multi-sensor complexity that using active signals was otherwise meant to avoid.
No gathered source addresses height or elevation for ultrasonic ranging.
A vertically-oriented array would be needed to get a z-axis reading at all, which is not demonstrated in the current evidence base.

*Pros:*

* Active-signal approach sidesteps the "room full of talking people" interference problem passive listening would face.
* Demonstrated real-time operation on embedded hardware (an FPGA, per Segers et al.).
* Reasonable literature base for ranging and positioning.

*Cons:*

* No gathered source addresses height or elevation for ultrasonic ranging.
* Coarse azimuth resolution compared to radar.
* Full coverage needs multiple synchronised nodes, reintroducing multi-sensor complexity.
* One of the pricier custom-built options (€200-400).

[[infrared-thermal]]
=== Infrared / Thermal

Low-resolution thermal/thermopile arrays measure a grid of per-pixel temperature readings.
A person would appear as a warm blob against the usually cooler background.
Resolution and range are both limited, which constrains this technology to smaller rooms, but the low pixel count is itself a privacy advantage.
The output is far below the resolution needed to identify a person or resolve recognisable imagery, which is a strong fit for the sub-question on minimising personally identifiable data from child visitors.

Most directly relevant to RQ1, Naser et al. (<<naser2021thermalheight,2021>>) explicitly extend thermal-array distance estimation to height measurement using a neural network.
This directly targets height estimation in the same way as this project's research question, and should be read first.
Feasibility on embedded hardware is demonstrated by Shubha and Shastrimath (<<shubha2022thermopile,2022>>), who report 99% occupancy accuracy using a Grid-EYE/AMG8833 thermopile array on a Raspberry Pi, addressing RQ2's embedded-platform requirement directly.
Foundational Grid-EYE-based detection and tracking work by Shetty et al. (<<shetty2017gridEye,2017>>) is extended by Klir et al. (<<klir2025thermopile,2025>>), who apply YOLOv5/DETR-style detection to 32x32 thermal images for multi-person localisation with a generative data model, which is relevant to the multi-person extensibility sub-question (RQ4).
Detection robustness under changing conditions is addressed by Trofimova et al. (<<trofimova-thermalbg,2017>>), who use adaptive background estimation for thermal-array-based human detection.

On latency, Naser et al. (<<naser2021thermalheight,2021>>) report the thermal sensor used in their height-estimation work supports a configurable sampling rate between 0.5 Hz and 64 Hz.
The specific Grid-EYE/AMG8833 breakout identified below for procurement is a simpler, coarser part by comparison: its datasheet exposes only a 10 Hz or 1 Hz frame-rate register, not the wider range Naser et al.'s sensor supports.
Shubha and Shastrimath (<<shubha2022thermopile,2022>>) are reported in secondary sources to have run their AMG8833/Raspberry Pi occupancy pipeline at 10 Hz, consistent with the datasheet's faster setting, though this could not be independently confirmed against the primary paper.

*Implication for RQ1:* thermal array has a source explicitly performing height estimation from sensor data, and its low resolution doubles as a privacy strength rather than only a limitation.
The main tradeoff to carry forward is its effective range, which needs to be checked against the actual dimensions of the interactive space.

Sensor hardware cost is substantially lower than mmWave, in the order of €40-55 excluding the embedded compute platform, based on the link:https://www.adafruit.com/product/3538[Adafruit AMG8833 breakout] list price, and the sensor interface (I2C) is simpler to bring up than a radar UART/configuration pipeline.

*Pros:*

* The only technology in this review with a source performing height estimation directly from sensor data (Naser et al.).
* Cheapest viable sensor-only option found (€40-55).
* Demonstrated real-time embedded operation on a Raspberry Pi (Shubha and Shastrimath).
* Low pixel count doubles as a privacy strength: the output cannot resolve recognisable imagery.
* Simpler sensor interface (I2C) than a radar UART/configuration pipeline.

*Cons:*

* Shorter effective range than mmWave, constraining it to smaller rooms.
* Lower spatial resolution overall, which needs checking against the Holobox's actual dimensions.

=== Capacitive & Floor-based Sensing

==== Capacitive / Electric-Field Sensing

Two device-free variants exist: proximity-plate sensing, which measures distance to a body via a change in capacitance, and floor-embedded capacitive electrodes, which require a person to be standing on the sensed area.

Ramezani Akhmareh et al. (<<ramezani2016tagless,2016>>) offer a good starting point for this sensing family, with a clear related-work review of alternative capacitive designs.
Faulkner et al.'s CapLoc (<<faulkner2020caploc,2020>>) is a floor-embedded capacitive sensing mat, building on the earlier CapFloor system, evaluated in the EvAAL indoor localisation competition for device-free localisation and fall detection.
The broadest source is Bian et al.'s survey (<<capacitiveSurvey2024,2024>>), covering proximity capacitive, floor capacitive, and passive electric-field sensing side by side.
A passive variant, Fu et al.'s Linoc system (<<fu2017linoc,2017>>), is reported as less noise-prone than active capacitive sensing, worth considering if EMI is a concern in the interaction space.

Range is the main practical constraint: plate sensors typically top out around one to two electrode diameters (roughly 0.3-1m), so whole-room coverage needs either a floor grid or many wall/ceiling nodes, good for near-field precision, not for single-sensor room-scale coverage.
The signal is also inherently a presence/proximity measure rather than a spatial one, so it is not naturally suited to a height reading at all.

*Implication for RQ1:* capacitive sensing has no reported height output, and its short range makes whole-room coverage costly without a dense sensor grid.

==== Floor-based (Pressure) Sensing

Floor-based sensing (pressure, piezoresistive, or capacitive-floor mats) is inherently a 2D floor-plane measurement, but because each footstep is a spatially discrete event, it naturally disambiguates multiple people.

Gatto et al. (<<gatto2015energyfloor,2015>>) use a 64x64 pressure-switch array per floor tile to track both humans and robots.
Al-Naimi et al. (<<alnaimi2014smartfloor,2014>>) fuse pressure-floor tracking with pyroelectric infrared sensors, and report the combination keeps working when individual people become hard to distinguish by pressure alone.
Feng et al. (<<feng2015fiberfloor,2015>>) use a fiber-optic floor grid explicitly built for multi-target localisation using group-testing theory, and Ibara et al. (<<ibara2013footsteps,2013>>) show gait-based individual identification from stride length and direction, noting extensibility to multi-person tracking.

*Implication for RQ1:* floor sensing's real strength is that it trivially solves "how many people, where" in a crowded room, since footsteps are naturally separated events.
Its weaknesses are installation cost (it has to go under the actual floor) and a complete absence of height information. Carrying this forward would mean pairing it with a separate height-sensing modality purely for the z-axis.

*Pros:*

* Floor-based variants trivially solve "how many people, where," since footsteps are naturally separated events.
* Solid literature base for both the capacitive and floor-pressure sub-variants.
* Inherently non-identifying (a presence/proximity or pressure signal, not imagery).

*Cons:*

* No height signal at all from either sub-variant.
* Capacitive sensing has short range, needing a dense grid for whole-room coverage.
* Floor-based sensing requires physical installation under the existing floor.
* Cost is highly variable and, for floor installations, can run into the thousands of euros.

[[uwb]]
=== Ultra-Wideband (UWB)

Device-free UWB sensing (IR-UWB) uses very short radio pulses across a wide bandwidth to estimate range and, via signal-processing or ML-based analysis of the reflected signal, detect and count people without requiring them to carry a tag.
Evidence gathered so far is limited to a single source: Njanda et al. (<<njanda2024uwbcount,2024>>) apply machine learning to IR-UWB radar for people counting.

The representative module named for this approach, the Novelda Xethru X4, does not have a single fixed frame rate in its own datasheet: frame rate directly trades off against SNR/processing gain (up to a maximum pulse repetition frequency of 40.5 MHz), so any specific frames-per-second figure is an application-specific configuration choice, not a hardware spec.
Njanda et al.'s paper itself could not be confirmed to report a frame rate or the specific radar hardware used.

*Implication for RQ1:* the evidence base for UWB is currently too thin to draw a conclusion for this project as no gathered source addresses height or pose-level output for UWB.
Given that UWB ranging chips are commonly cheap and embedded-friendly, this is flagged for a further literature pass before being ruled in or out, rather than being ruled out on the current single source.

*Pros:*

* People-counting literature exists (Njanda et al., reporting 99% accuracy with an ANN classifier).
* Radar-based output is inherently non-identifying.

*Cons:*

* No height or pose-level literature at all; flagged in this review as unproven.
* Only a single source exists, described in this chapter as too thin an evidence base to draw a conclusion from.
* The representative module identified (Novelda Xethru X4) is no longer available through an authorised distributor, making cost and sourcing unverifiable.

[[multi-person-speaker]]
== Multi-Person: Identifying the Active Speaker

Everything above addresses single-person position and height.
A separate problem, relevant to RQ4's extensibility to multi-person scenarios, is that once several people are present in the interactive space, the system also needs to determine which one is currently speaking to the Virtual Human, without a camera, and without relying on people carrying a microphone or tag.

=== mmWave Radar: Resolution Limits for Counting, Height, and Speaker Separation

Whether the IWR6843 reports two people as distinct targets, rather than merging them into one detection, depends on the radar's angular and range resolution, not on a single blanket "multi-person" capability.
Two people need roughly 1.7-2.9m of horizontal (left-right) separation, or a height difference on the order of 1.7m (e.g. one seated, one standing), before the radar's angular resolution reliably reports them as two separate people instead of one merged detection.
Range separation (front-back, i.e. distance from the sensor) is far finer, on the order of 4-5cm, and follows automatically from the radar's time-of-flight measurement rather than needing angular discrimination, so it is not a practical constraint in the way horizontal or height separation is.

Speaker identification does not share this constraint.
It relies on Doppler micro-motion detection, the small, characteristic mouth/chest movements produced during speech (the same underlying mechanism as the mmWave vibrometry work cited below), rather than on spatial separation between people.
This means the radar can identify who is speaking regardless of how close together multiple people are standing, even in cases where their positions are too close to separate cleanly by angle.

For a room roughly 5m wide with 2-3 people scattered across it, ordinary spacing is sufficient to satisfy all three requirements together: counting people, estimating each person's height, and identifying the active speaker.
The failure case to plan around is 4 or more people clustered tightly together, where horizontal separation drops below the 1.7-2.9m angular-resolution threshold and individual tracks become ambiguous, the same underlying failure mode as the "crossover ambiguity" already noted for 2D LiDAR above, rather than a new risk specific to mmWave.

=== mmWave Vibrometry

mmWave radar's vibrometry capability, established above for position and pose sensing, extends to sound itself: Ozturk et al. (<<ozturk2021radiomic,2021>>) show that mmWave radar can reconstruct audio from the surface vibrations of a sound source, including a human throat, working even through soundproofing.
More directly on point, Xu et al. (<<xu2019waveear,2019>>) build WaveEar, a system that uses a low-cost mmWave probe to first localise the position of the speaker among multiple people, then steer the radar toward that person's throat to sense their vocal vibrations.
This effectively solves "who is speaking" and "what are they saying" from the same non-camera sensor.

*Implication for RQ4:* mmWave vibrometry can localise a speaker among multiple people and recover their voice from the same radar signal.
Neither source has been tested in this project's specific context (children's voices, a room-scale interactive installation), so this is flagged for a dedicated literature and bench-testing pass.

=== Wi-Fi CSI Sensing

Wi-Fi CSI reflections from mouth and lip movement during speech are detectable without any additional hardware beyond commodity Wi-Fi.
Wang et al.'s WiHear (<<wang2014wihear,2014>>) uses directional antennas aimed at a target's face to detect fine-grained radio reflections from mouth movement, and reports being able to sense multiple people's talk simultaneously using MIMO.
Du et al.'s WiTalk (<<du2018witalk,2018>>) reports over 92% recognition accuracy discerning a set of syllables from WiFi CSI dynamics alone.

*Implication for RQ4:* Wi-Fi CSI can detect and analyse mouth-movement reflections associated with speech using only commodity Wi-Fi hardware.
Neither source has been tested in this project's specific context (children's voices, a room-scale interactive installation), so this is flagged for a dedicated literature and bench-testing pass.

=== UWB Radar

IR-UWB radar can remotely detect motion of the lips and jaw associated with speech.
Shin and Seo (<<shin2016uwbsilent,2016>>) extract lip and jaw motion features from IR-UWB radar signals for contactless silent speech recognition, improving recognition performance over using raw radar signal features directly.

*Implication for RQ4:* IR-UWB radar can detect articulator motion associated with speech from radar reflections alone.
This source has not been tested in this project's specific context (children's voices, a room-scale interactive installation), so this is flagged for a dedicated literature and bench-testing pass.

=== Laser Doppler Vibrometry

A laser Doppler vibrometer (LDV) measures skin-surface vibration at a distance via the Doppler shift of a reflected laser beam, and can pick up throat vibration during speech even in acoustically noisy conditions.
Avargel and Cohen (<<avargel2011ldv,2011>>) combine an LDV sensor aimed at the larynx with a standard microphone, using a pre-filtering and voice-activity-detection algorithm to enhance speech quality under transient noise.

*Implication for RQ4:* LDV can pick up a specific person's throat vibration with high immunity to ambient acoustic noise, but it requires the laser to be aimed precisely at that person's throat, and several published LDV setups rely on a camera to aim and track the target, which would need a non-camera aiming mechanism to fit this project's constraints.
This source has not been tested in this project's specific context (children's voices, a room-scale interactive installation), so this is flagged for a dedicated literature and bench-testing pass.

=== Microphone Array (Passive Acoustic)

Berghi and Jackson (<<berghi2023speakerloc,2023>>) use a microphone array with a convolutional recurrent neural network to detect and localise an active talker's horizontal position from multichannel audio alone, comparing GCC-PHAT, SALSA features, and beamforming as input representations.
This requires no camera or radar hardware at all, only an array of microphones placed in the interactive space.

*Implication for RQ4:* a microphone array can localise an active talker's position from audio alone, with no line-of-sight or radar hardware requirement.
It has not been tested in this project's specific context (children's voices, a room-scale interactive installation), so this is flagged for a dedicated literature and bench-testing pass.

[[combined-approaches]]
== Combined Sensor Approaches

The individual technologies above are also weighed as sensor combinations, where pairing two of them addresses a gap neither has alone.

[[combo-mmwave-thermal]]
=== mmWave + Thermal Fusion for Height

The project's own risk analysis anticipates that a single sensor may not reliably discriminate height across the full range, and names combining "two or more complementary sensors (fusion)" as the planned fallback.
The concrete instantiation of that plan is to add the infrared/thermal array as a second, height-specific sensor alongside mmWave, rather than replacing mmWave outright: mmWave continues to provide position and person tracking, while the thermal array's independent height reading, backed by Naser et al. (<<naser2021thermalheight,2021>>), is used to confirm, or substitute for, mmWave's own height estimate.

This is a narrower, lower-risk form of fusion than <<combo-lidar-thermal,2D LiDAR + Thermal Fusion>> below: that combination requires fusing two independent position-sensing streams from scratch, whereas this option keeps mmWave as the sole position sensor and only adds a second signal for the specific sub-problem, height.
The main added implementation work is registering the thermal array's field of view against the mmWave-detected person's position, rather than fusing two full tracking pipelines.
Combined sensor-only hardware cost is the sum of the mmWave and thermal entries in <<sensor-comparison-summary>>, roughly €200-240, plus a single shared compute platform.

*Pros:*

* Combines mmWave's strong position/multi-person tracking with thermal's direct height-estimation precedent.
* Gives a concrete fallback if mmWave's own height estimate underperforms, without discarding the mmWave investment.
* Reuses the same thermal hardware already procured in the prototyping plan's first phase, rather than adding a third sensor.

*Cons:*

* Requires registering the thermal array's field of view against the mmWave-detected person's position: real added implementation work.
* Combined cost still includes the pricier mmWave component (€200-240 total).
* No single published source tests this exact combination; each half is validated separately, not together.

[[combo-budget-tier]]
=== Budget Tier: HLK-LD2450 + Thermal Fusion

A further budget tier exists by swapping the position sensor itself.
The link:https://shillehtek.com/products/hlk-ld2450-24ghz-mmwave-radar-human-body-tracking-sensor-module[HLK-LD2450] is a 24 GHz radar module, around €8-12, that tracks X/Y position and speed for up to three people simultaneously over UART, with a 6 m range and a 10 Hz update rate.
Paired with the same infrared/thermal array for height, following the same fusion pattern as above, combined sensor-only hardware cost drops to roughly €50-70, a fraction of either mmWave-based option.

This option carries a materially different kind of risk than the rest of this chapter.
Every other candidate technology evaluated here is backed by at least one peer-reviewed source; no such source was found for the HLK-LD2450.
It is documented only through the manufacturer's own manual and hobbyist/smart-home community projects (e.g. Home Assistant and ESPHome integrations), not academic literature.
Choosing it would mean the position-tracking half of the system rests entirely on this project's own bench validation rather than on prior published results.
That is a different kind of unknown than "will the accuracy be good enough": it is "has anyone shown this works for this purpose at all."
Like 2D LiDAR, it also only outputs a single scan-plane (x, y) position, so it inherits the same reliance on a second sensor for height.

*Pros:*

* Dramatically cheaper than either mmWave-based option (€50-70 total, sensor-only).
* Still gets a height reading via the same literature-backed thermal array.
* The HLK-LD2450 already tracks up to three people's X/Y position and speed out of the box.

*Cons:*

* No peer-reviewed source exists for the HLK-LD2450; it is documented only through the manufacturer's manual and hobbyist community projects.
* The position-tracking half of the system would rest entirely on this project's own bench validation, not prior published results.
* Only proposed as a fallback of last resort, gated on a standalone feasibility test before being trusted.

[[combo-lidar-thermal]]
=== 2D LiDAR + Thermal Fusion

*Pros:*

* 2D LiDAR contributes strong in-plane position accuracy and occlusion tolerance (Bouazizi et al.).
* Thermal contributes the same direct height-estimation precedent used elsewhere in this chapter (Naser et al.).

*Cons:*

* The most expensive combination reviewed in this chapter (€290-345, sensor-only).
* Combining two independent position-sensing streams from scratch introduces synchronisation and fusion complexity flagged as a disproportionate implementation risk for this project's timeline, per xref:../../project-plan/approach-planning/timeline.adoc[Timeline].
* Unlike the mmWave + Thermal option, this does not reuse an already-working single-sensor pipeline.

[[recommended-approach]]
== Recommended Approach: mmWave, Phased by Capability

On the criteria set out earlier, mmWave radar comes out ahead: it is the only approach with keypoint-level pose literature, it extends to the RQ4 speaker-identification problem via the same sensor, its output is inherently non-identifying, and it has the strongest literature base of any candidate reviewed.
The infrared/thermal array remains the only approach with a direct height-estimation precedent and by far the cheapest to acquire, but on technical merit mmWave is the stronger choice, and it is recommended directly rather than deferred behind a cheaper sensor.
This is a recommendation of the mmWave modality; which specific IWR6843 evaluation-board variant to build with is a separate, narrower decision, addressed next in <<mmwave-module-selection,mmWave Module Selection>>.

mmWave is, by a wide margin, still the most expensive candidate reviewed (roughly three to four times the thermal array's sensor cost), and no project budget has yet been agreed with MindLabs.
That cost is treated as a precondition to clear with the company mentor before hardware is ordered, not as a reason to build something else first.
What is staged instead is the implementation itself: rather than assuming position tracking, height estimation, multi-person clustering, and speaker detection all work together on the first attempt, the mmWave pipeline is built up one capability at a time, each on top of an already-working previous phase.
This still isolates risk (a problem in one capability does not require redoing the whole pipeline) without introducing a second sensor and a mid-project hardware swap.
If mmWave's own height estimate underperforms once measured, <<combo-mmwave-thermal,mmWave + Thermal Fusion for Height>> remains the documented fallback; if the mmWave budget cannot be approved at all, <<combo-budget-tier,the HLK-LD2450 budget tier>> is the fallback of last resort.

The concrete phasing of this capability build-up is carried into the Conclusion and the Prototyping Plan chapter.
